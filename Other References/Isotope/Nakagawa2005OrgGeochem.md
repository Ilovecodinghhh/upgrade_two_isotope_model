Abstract
The stable carbon and hydrogen isotopic compositions (d13C and dD, respectively) of methane (CH4) in automobile
exhaust were determined in order to quantify by mass balance the contribution from this source to atmospheric CH4 in
an urban area of Nagoya, Japan. Both d13C and dD values of CH4 in automobile exhaust increased in accordance with
the model year of the engine, probably as a result of isotope fractionation eﬀects associated with oxidation over metal
catalysts in the catalytic converter of modern vehicles. Thus, CH4 emissions from recent automobiles exhibit conspic-
uous 13C and D enrichment compared to the ﬂux from other major anthropogenic sources, such as natural gas leakage,
landﬁlls and rice paddies. Using average d13C and dD CH4 values estimated for local sources, automobile exhaust in
Nagoya, Japan, was determined to contribute signiﬁcant amounts (up to 30%) of CH4 to the troposphere in the study
area.

atmospheric CH4 has increased rapidly, more than dou-

(Craig and Chou, 1982; Etheridge et al., 1998; Pearman

egies for controlling CH4 emissions to the atmosphere,
the strength of anthropogenic sources needs to be accu-
rately assessed.
Highly populated urban areas host many anthropo-
genic sources of CH4 and, as a result, accumulation of
CH4 emitted from local sources (within several tens of
kilometers) typically occurs at night during nocturnal
stratiﬁcation of the atmosphere. During the daytime,
the local concentration of atmosphere CH4 usually re-
turns to the background level normally found in the free
troposphere (Lowry et al., 2001; Moriizumi et al., 1996).
The magnitude of anthropogenic CH4 emissions in ur-
ban environments shows rapid and large temporal vari-
ations and trends on a scale of a few years to decades
(Ito et al., 2000; Lowry et al., 2001) because of changes
in parameters, such as population size and distribution,
types of energy resources, methods of waste disposal,
and lifestyle. It is important to clarify the main temporal
contributor to urban atmospheric CH4 because it may
help to predict future emission levels in a given urban
area.
Automobile exhaust is one of the known sources of
atmospheric CH4 in urban environments, but land use
in such areas generally is too complex to allow local
contributions from automobiles to be estimated by
measurement of the CH4 tropospheric mixing ratio at
a limited number of ﬁxed stations. However, the stable
isotopic composition of CH4 is a useful tracer for iden-
tifying emission sources and constraining ﬂux strength
at both the local (Kuc et al., 2003; Levin et al., 1993;
Yamada et al., 2003) and global scale (Quay et al.,
1999) via isotope mass balance models. However, eval-
uating source strengths of atmospheric CH4 using 13C
and D as tracers requires a knowledge of d13C and
dD CH4 values from representative emission sources.
To date, dD values of CH4 from automobile exhaust
have yet to be reported and to our knowledge, pioneer-
ing studies have been conducted only for 13C (Chanton
et al., 2000; Levin et al., 1999). Consequently, we deter-
mined d13C and dD values of CH4 from automobile ex-
haust in Nagoya City to evaluate the contribution of
automobile emissions to tropospheric CH4 levels in
an urban area using isotope mass balance. Other local
sources include natural gas leakage, rice paddies and a
landﬁll.
1.2. Study area
The evaluation of automobile exhaust in an urban
atmosphere was conducted in Nagoya City, the fourth
largest city in Japan, having a population of more than
two million. In the city, temporal variations in atmo-
spheric CH4 abundance have been monitored at several
stations (Ito et al., 2000, 2001; Moriizumi et al., 1996).
These studies indicated that urban areas in Nagoya are
a signiﬁcant net source of atmospheric CH4, having an
areal ﬂux ranging from 0.01 to 0.02 g CH4 m2 d1
(Moriizumi et al., 1996). By comparing spatial and tem-
poral variations in urban atmospheric CH4 with meteo-
rological conditions, it has been demonstrated that local
landﬁlls contribute signiﬁcant amounts of CH4 to the ur-
ban atmosphere in Nagoya (Ito et al., 2001). In another
study, which measured the 14C content of tropospheric
CH4 to estimate the contribution of fossil (14C-free)
sources to the urban atmosphere, it was concluded that
natural gas was the dominant source of atmospheric
CH4 in urban areas (Moriizumi et al., 1998). However,
the contribution of automobile exhaust, which is an-
other possible source of fossil CH4 emissions to the
atmosphere, has yet to be evaluated and is the focus of
this study. In addition, a suburban area of Nagoya City
was studied for comparison.
2. Methods
2.1. Atmospheric samples
Air sampling was performed at three sites in Nagoya
City: Higashi-Sakura (Stn-H), Tomida (Stn-T), and Na-
goya University (Stn-N; (Fig. 1). Stn-H is located in
downtown Nagoya City. Stn-T is situated in a suburban
area where rice paddies are farmed near houses. Stn-N is
located on the eastern side of the city, which consists
mainly of residences.
Air samples were collected at Stn-H in the winter
(November and December) of 1993, at Stn-T in summer
(July and August) of 1994 and at Stn-N in both summer
(July 1993) and winter (November 1993 and January
and February 1994). Samples were collected both before
sunrise and during the daytime in order to quantify vari-
ations in the isotopic composition of atmospheric CH4
associated with its diurnal ﬂuctuation in abundance.
At Stn-H and Stn-T, air from a height of 1.5 m above
the ground was pumped into a large Tedlar bag (polyvi-
nyl ﬂuoride; 1.1 m3 STP). At Stn-N, a similar volume of
air was sampled at a height of 10 m using the same

2.2. Automobile exhaust
Automobile exhaust was collected from the tailpipes
of idling automobiles to determine d13C and dD values
of CH4 in exhaust gases. Three gasoline automobiles of
diﬀerent age were selected to evaluate the inﬂuence of
engine combustion eﬃciency and/or the eﬀects of cata-
lytic converters in the sample vehicles: (1) 1966 Volkswa-
gen Type 1 (CAR-66), a very old automobile without a
catalytic converter; (2) 1993 Nissan Primera (CAR-93),
a recent automobile with a catalytic converter; (3) 2002
Honda Fit (CAR-02), a brand new automobile with a
catalytic converter. Automobile exhaust was sampled

Tygon tube (6-mm i.d. · 0.3-m length). After purging

were closed. Seven to nine gas samples were taken from
each automobile.

The d13C and dD values of atmospheric CH4 varied
to some extent at each site (Table 2). The largest varia-
tions of 8.6& for d13C and 101& for dD occurred at
Stn-T. Fig. 6 shows the linear relationships that existed
between the isotope composition of atmospheric meth-

At all sites, d13C and dD values of atmospheric CH4 ap-
proached the known tropospheric background values
(d13C = 47.2 to 47.6& and dD = 80 to 95&;
Quay et al., 1999) when the CH4 mixing ratio decreased
to background levels. The d13C and dD CH4 values var-
ied at higher mixing ratios because CH4 emissions from
local sources have diﬀerent stable isotope compositions
from background tropospheric CH4.
Atmospheric CH4 was D-depleted at higher concen-
trations, suggesting that accumulated CH4 from local
sources had low dD values. This ﬁnding is consistent
with the D-depleted values for CH4 discussed in Section
3.1. A negative correlation between dD CH4 values and
CH4 abundance also can be explained by isotopic frac-
tionation associated with CH4 oxidation by OH radicals
in the troposphere (Cantrell et al., 1990). However, this
process is unlikely to be important in short term obser-
vations because of the 10 year lifetime of CH4 in the
troposphere. Therefore, simple mixing between local
sources with low dD values and background CH4 is
the most plausible explanation of variations in atmo-
spheric dD CH4 values in Nagoya.
The relationship between the stable carbon isotope
composition of CH4 and its concentration diﬀered
among sites and seasons, suggesting that the predomi-
nant sources of atmospheric CH4 diﬀered spatially and
temporally. Generally, 13C enrichment with increasing
CH4 concentration was observed in winter, whereas
13C depletion was observed in summer. This ﬁnding sug-
gests that fossil CH4 sources, such as natural gas leakage
or automobile exhaust, were the dominant sources of
atmospheric CH4 in winter, while contributions from
biogenic sources increased during the summer. A quan-
titative evaluation of this speculation is provided below.
