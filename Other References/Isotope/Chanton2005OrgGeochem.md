Abstract
This paper reviews the 13C isotopic fractionations of the three main modes of CH4 transport from wetlands: diﬀusion, ebul-
lition and via emergent aquatic plants. Aquatic plants employ either diﬀusion or a combination of convective bulk ﬂow in day-
light and diﬀusion at night. Ebullition and diﬀusion across the air–water interface result in no to little isotopic fractionation.
However, diﬀusion through air when conducted by plant lacunae or aerenchyma results in signiﬁcantly greater rates of trans-
port for 12CH4 relative to 13CH4. This diﬀerence causes the backup of 13C-enriched CH4 in plant lacunae, which partly but not
totally compensates for the isotopic fractionation out of the plant. It is also suggested that the preferential plant transport of
12CH4 causes 13C enrichment of methane in the rhizosphere but it is diﬃcult to separate the eﬀects of rhizospheric methane
oxidation from this process. Convective bulk ﬂow transport via plants results in little isotopic fractionation. However, the pat-
tern of fractionation diﬀers signiﬁcantly in convective plants from day to night as diﬀusion is more important in darkness.

1. Use of stable isotopes
Stable isotopes are extremely useful for diagnosing a
wide variety of processes associated with determining
methane production mechanisms (Whiticar et al., 1986;
Whiticar, 1993, 1999; Hornibrook et al., 1997, 2000;
Lansdown et al., 1992), the extent of methane oxidation
(Barker and Fritz, 1981; Coleman et al., 1981) or track-
ing sources of methane (Martens et al., 1991). Applica-
tions vary across a wide variety of scales. On a small
scale, shifts in methane d13C can be used to calculate
fractional oxidation in the transport of methane across
landﬁll and wetlands soils (Borjesson et al., 2001; Happ-
ell et al., 1994). On a global scale, the isotopic composi-

methods proved to be a key.’’
2. Three modes of methane transport from wetlands
Methane is transported from anoxic sediments and
ﬂooded soils by three main pathways: molecular diﬀu-
sion, bubble ebullition and via emergent plants (Schutz
et al., 1991, Fig. 1). The relative importance of these
three pathways varies according to organic loading, sea-
sonal variation in temperature and the type and density
of aquatic vegetation.
Molecular diﬀusion is the random movement of mol-
ecules down a concentration gradient, from higher to
lower concentration. Rates of diﬀusion are regulated
by the concentration gradient, the diﬀusion coeﬃcient
and, in sediments and soils, the porosity. Diﬀusion fol-
lows Ficks ﬁrst law (Berner, 1980):

Molecular diﬀusion is important in the transport of

cover is mainly sphagnum (Chasar et al., 2000a,b; Fech-

Bubble ebullition occurs because methane is only
sparingly soluble in water (Yamamoto et al., 1976). Ebul-
lition transports methane directly to the atmosphere,
bypassing the mediating eﬀects of an oxygenated water
column or plant rhizome. In the process of sedimentary
bubble formation, dissolved methane and other gases
equilibrate with the bubble reservoir in the sediment
and are subsequently stripped from solution as bubbles
are released (Kipphut and Martens, 1982; Chanton
et al., 1989; Martens and Chanton, 1989). Bubble N2
content can serve as an indicator of the importance of
diﬀering gas transport mechanisms (e.g., ebullition ver-
sus plant transport, Boon and Sorrell, 1991; Sorrell and
Boon, 1992) or may be indicative of rates of ebullition
from sediments (Chanton et al., 1989; Martinova,
1998). As increasing volumes of methane bubble from
the sediments, they strip out N2, a gas that is not pro-
duced within the sediments. Thus, bubbles with low N2
concentration indicate high rates of bubble stripping,
while bubbles with high N2 concentration indicate that
little ebullition has removed N2 from the sediment.

Aquatic plants conduct considerable quantities of
methane as natural wetlands and artiﬁcial wetlands (rice
ﬁelds) are two of the largest sources of methane in the
atmosphere (Cicerone and Oremland, 1988; Reeburgh,
2003). Many emergent aquatic macrophytes have large
interior open spaces, termed lacunaeor aerenchyma, with-
in their culms, stems or petioles (Dacey, 1981a,b). These
plants release methane from the sediments while trans-
porting oxygen to support the respiration of their roots
and rhizomes, which often live in organic-rich anoxic sed-
iments and soils (Schutz et al., 1991; Chanton and Dacey,
1991; Shannon and White, 1994; Shannon et al., 1996;
King et al., 1998). In comparisons of vegetated areas with
adjacent non-vegetated areas, it has been found that
plants generally transport 10 times the amount of CH4 rel-
ative to the non-vegetated interface (Whiting et al., 1991;
Whiting and Chanton, 1992; Happell et al., 1993).
Ventilation of CH4 by plants can aﬀect gas transport
mode by reducing methane partial pressures within the
ﬂooded soil or sediment in which they are rooted. Where
plants are present, pore water methane concentrations
are roughly 50% lower due to plant ventilation of the
soil and this in turn reduces the methane concentration
gradient out of the soil, reducing diﬀusive ﬂux (Wilson
et al., 1989; Chanton et al., 1989; Chanton and Dacey,
1991). Another eﬀect of reducing pore water CH4 con-
centrations is to reduce CH4 bubble concentration and
total bubble volume, both of which reduce ebullition
rates. Thus, if vascular emergent macrophyte plants in-
habit a wetland, plant transport will be the primary
mechanism of CH4 transport from the wetland (Van
der Nat and Middelburg, 1998). In vegetated areas,
CH4 transport is dominated by plants while in non-
vegetated ones CH4 transport is dominated by ebullition
and diﬀusion (Kaki et al., 2001; Tyler et al., 1997; Boon
and Sorrell, 1991). Macrophyte population is controlled

Aquatic macrophytes may employ passive (diﬀusive)
gas transport systems or active gas ﬂow systems (Dacey
and Klug, 1979; Dacey, 1981a,b; Armstrong and Arm-
strong, 1990, 1991; Grosse et al., 1991, 1996; Schutz

Passive (diﬀusive) transport is the sole gas transport
mode found in shallow water plants such as Carex ros-
trata, Oryza sativa and Peltandra virginica (Chanton
and Dacey, 1991; Van Bodegom et al., 2001).
In deeper water, plants that employ diﬀusive transport
are at a competitive disadvantage relative to plants that
use convective through-ﬂow ventilation. Diﬀusion is less
adequate for spanning longer distances between the atmo-
sphere and the roots, so active modes of gas transport give
certain plants an advantage in deeper water (Grosse et al.,
1991). Sorrell et al. (1997) found that for Eleocharis sph-
acelata diﬀusion is inadequate for satisfying oxygen de-
mands in plants growing in more than 10 cm of water
and that convection is essential in the natural habitat of
the species. Convective inﬂows in this plant increased with
greater water depth (Sorrell and Tanner, 2000).
Active transport is the convective through-ﬂow of
gases from areas of high pressure to low pressure. The
pressure gradient can be created by either thermal tran-
spiration (Dacey, 1981a,b; Grosse et al., 1991; Grosse,
1996) or it can be induced from a humidity diﬀerential
across the leaf boundary (Armstrong and Armstrong,
1990, 1991; Armstrong et al., 1992, 1996a,b; Brix
et al., 1992, 1996; Sorrell and Boon, 1994; Sorrell
et al., 1997; Arkebauer et al., 2001). Pressurization of
young green leaves causes through-ﬂow to porous dead
or senescent leaves (Dacey, 1981a,b; Armstrong et al.,
1992; Bendix et al., 1994). Pressurization sets up a gas
ﬂow through the plant culms or petioles as illustrated
in Fig. 2 for Phragmites australis. Gas ﬂow rates of 10
mL/min were observed by Chanton et al. (2002).
Plants that utilize convective through-ﬂow transport
include Typha latifolia, Typha angustifolia, Nymphaea
odorata, Nuphar luteum, Nelumbo nucifera, Nymphoides
pelata, Eleocharis sphacelata and Phragmites australis.
Convective through-ﬂow transport generally occurs in
the daylight (Dacey, 1981a,b; Grosse and Mevi-Schutz,
1987; Mevi-Schutz and Grosse, 1988; Armstrong and
Armstrong, 1991; Bendix et al., 1994; Brix et al., 1992,
1996; Tornbjerg et al., 1994; Arkebauer et al., 2001). At
night, when pressure gradients within the plants are ab-
sent, molecular diﬀusion is the primary transport mecha-
nism (references above). However, small increases in CH4
ﬂux on windy nights have been contributed to Venturi-
induced convection in Phragmites australis (Armstrong
et al., 1992, 1996a; Brix et al., 1992; Kim et al., 1998).

4. Isotopic fractionation by diﬀusion
Isotopic fractionation caused by transport processes
is important for characterizing the isotopic value of
the methane ﬂux from particular environments and for
revealing subtleties in the transport processes. Addition-
ally, patterns of isotopic fractionation are used to deter-
mine methane oxidation (Happell et al., 1994; Kelley
et al., 1995; Lombardi et al., 1997; Tyler et al., 1997;
Bilek et al., 1999; Popp et al., 2000) and methane produc-
tion (Whiticar et al., 1986; Whiticar, 1999; Lansdown
et al., 1992; Kelley et al., 1992, 1995; Martens et al.,
1992; Hornibrook et al., 1997, 2000; Chanton et al.,
2005). The fractionations associated with gas transport
can obscure and confuse interpretation of these latter
two biological processes.

4.3. Fractionation when diﬀusive supply limits consumption
Diﬀusion of methane into sub aerial soils is a diﬀerent
case. Methane is consumed within forest soils by methan-
otrophic bacteria that have aﬃnity for the very low con-
centration in the atmosphere, i.e., 1.8 ppmv (Born et al.,
1990; Keller et al., 1990; Crill, 1991). Methane is supplied
to these soil bacteria by diﬀusion from the atmosphere
into the soil. Often the bacteria are associated with the soil
mineral layer that is several centimeters below more or-
ganic-rich layers (Crill, 1991). In an elegant study, Tyler
et al. (1994a) observed that the rate of microbial consump-
tion of methane was limited by the diﬀusion of methane
into the soil. Since all of the methane that diﬀused into
the forest soil was consumed, they noted that the diﬀer-
ences in the diﬀusion coeﬃcients of methane controlled
the total isotopic fractionation associated with forest soil
methane uptake, not the enzymatic fractionation of the
oxidizing bacteria. The bacteria quantitatively consumed
all of the methane that reached them without discrimina-
tion. The fractionation occurred in the transport to them
and the isotopic composition of what reached them was
controlled by the relative diﬀerences in the diﬀusion coef-
ﬁcients (D12/D13 = 1.0195), as described above. However,
while the fractionation by diﬀusive transport is relatively
well deﬁned, variation in fractionation factors for meth-
ane oxidation by microbial process remains relatively
undeﬁned (Reeburgh, 1996) and may vary with CH4 con-
centration and temperature (Reeburgh et al., 1997). Ree-
burgh et al. (1997) used three approaches to estimate the
biological fractionation of methane uptake in forest soils
and determined that the overall isotopic fractionation was
controlled by a combination of transport processes and
microbial discrimination.

6. Isotopic fractionation associated with transport by
aquatic plants
6.1. Gas transport modes
There are three modes of gas transport by which
methane is transported out of a plant: (1) eﬀusion, also
called free-molecular or Knudsen ﬂow, which can be de-
scribed as the passage of gas in the presence of a pressure
diﬀerential, through a partition with holes which are
small relative to the mean free path of a gas molecule
in air, (2) viscous, bulk or convective ﬂow, in which
the gas acts as a continuum driven by a pressure gradi-
ent, or (3) diﬀusion, described by Ficks law, in which
the gas responds to the inﬂuence of a composition or
partial pressure gradient in the absence of a total pres-
sure gradient (Dacey, 1987; Mason and Evans, 1969;
Mason and Marrero, 1970).
Of these three mechanisms, only the second, bulk or
convective ﬂow, does not result in a molecular weight
dependent fractionation. This kind of ﬂow takes place
in the transfer from the inlet system to the ionization
chamber in the isotope ratio mass spectrometer. Eﬀusion
is the principle behind the separation of 235U from 238U
in the gas phase as hexaﬂorides. This technique was used
by Ramsey to separate He from N2 through unglazed
clay piping (Kennard, 1938). Dacey (1987) was able to
characterize pore sizes in living leaves of Nelumbo using
fractionation of N2 and Ar in eﬀusion experiments. Sch-
roder (1989) demonstrated fractionation of ethane and
propane during gas transport in the black alder in order
to estimate pore sizes in lenticel tissues. Harden and
Chanton (1994) used ethane and propane fractionation
to demonstrate diﬀusive fractionation in gas transport
from Pontideria cordata (Pickerel weed) and Sagittaria
lancifolia (Bull tongue). Van der Nat et al. (1998) used
ﬂux variations under diﬀering chamber atmospheres to
deduce gas transport mechanisms.

molecular weight of a gas according to Grahams law

6.2. Patterns of isotopic fractionation
In the mid 1980s, a number of research groups char-
acterized the d13C value of atmospheric methane sources
(Stevens and Engelkemeir, 1988; Cicerone and Orem-
land, 1988; Tyler, 1989, 1991) to constrain estimates of
the relative quantities of the inputs that were fueling
the rapid (1% per year) increase in atmospheric methane
(Cicerone and Oremland, 1988). It was hypothesized
that methane transported out of the sediments by emer-
gent aquatic plants would be very enriched in 13C and D
due to rhizospheric methane oxidation (Chanton et al.,
1988). Signiﬁcant rates of methane oxidation occur
when material from the rhizosphere is incubated with
methane under O2 (King, 1990, 1994; Chanton et al.,
1992b; Gerard and Chanton, 1993; Moore et al., 1994;
Moore and Dalva, 1997). Methanotrophic bacteria in
the rhizosphere were envisioned as being a gauntlet that

within the lacunal spaces of Peltandra virginica, Arcto-
