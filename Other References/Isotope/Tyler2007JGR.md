Stable isotope ratios in atmospheric CH 4:
Implications for seasonal sources and sinks
Stanley C. Tyler,1 Andrew L. Rice,2,3,4 and Henry O. Ajie1
Received 2 March 2006; revised 31 July 2006; accepted 7 September 2006; published 6 February 2007.
[1]
We report dD and d13C measurements of atmospheric CH4 from air samples collected
from two locations in the United States. They are the mid continental site Niwot Ridge,
Colorado (40N, 105W), and a Pacific coastal site receiving strong westerlies, Montan˜a
de Oro, California (35N, 121W). Data from multiyear approximately bimonthly
sampling provide information relating seasonal cycling of CH4 sources and sinks in
background air, record long-term trends in CH4 mixing and isotope ratio related to the
atmospheric CH4 loading, and may indicate regional CH4 sources. At Niwot Ridge,
dD-CH4 averaged 93.1 ± 3.0% from 1999 to 2001, while d13C-CH4 averaged
47.22 ± 0.13% from 1995 to 2001 with distinct seasonal cycles in both isotope ratios. At
Montan˜a de Oro, atmospheric CH4 was observed to be more depleted in 13C and D:
Measured dD-CH4 averaged 97.3 ± 3.7% from 2000 to 2001, while d13C-CH4 averaged
47.26 ± 0.17% from 1996 to 2001, and seasonal cycles were larger than those
observed at Niwot Ridge. Mixing ratios observed at Montan˜a de Oro were higher on
average than at Niwot Ridge. At both sites, d13C-CH4 was found to correlate poorly with
mixing ratio, an indication that varying CH4 sources are partly responsible for the
d13C-CH4 seasonal signal. In contrast, a strong anticorrelation exists between dD-CH4 and
mixing ratio, with maxima and minima approximately 6 months out of phase, indicating a
sensitivity of dD to sink processes. The dual isotopic constraint to atmospheric CH4
seasonality implies that these midlatitude sites are annually influenced by a 13C-enriched
CH4 source(s) seasonally increasing in late spring and a 13C-depleted CH4 source(s)
seasonally increasing in late summer or early fall.

1.
Introduction
[2] Methane (CH4), the most abundant hydrocarbon in
the atmosphere, plays an important role in tropospheric and
stratospheric chemistry [e.g., Cicerone and Oremland,
1988]. It reacts with hydroxyl radical (OH), acting as a
control on the oxidizing power of the atmosphere and
affecting many trace species such as carbon monoxide
(CO), ozone (O3), formaldehyde, atmospheric hydrogen,
and water vapor. It also reacts with Cl, where it sequesters
Cl from taking part in O3 destruction in the stratosphere.
Methane is also an important greenhouse gas [Ramanathan
et al., 1985; Dickinson and Cicerone, 1986] with a
concentration known to be increasing on a global scale
since preindustrial times [e.g., Blake and Rowland, 1988;
Dlugokencky et al., 1994, 1998; Etheridge et al., 1998].
Although this increase has been variable on a year to year
basis in recent decades, it is persistent and the specific
reasons for either increases or decreases in its growth rate
are not fully understood [e.g., Dlugokencky et al., 2001,
2003]. It is widely believed that the increasing concentration
of CH4 and other greenhouse gases affect climate, and that
if the present emission trends continue, global temperature
will rise [Hansen et al., 2000; Ehhalt et al., 2001; Shindell
et al., 2005].
[3] Stable isotope information provides a major constraint
on CH4 sources and sink processes. Stevens and Rust [1982]
first proposed that a mass-weighted stable carbon isotopic
balance between CH4 sources and sink processes and the
d13C-CH4 value in the atmosphere would help constrain the
CH4 budget. (Stable isotope measurement results are nearly
always expressed in d notation as in the equation d13C =
[(13C/12C)sam/(13C/12C)std  1]  1000, using carbon as an
example. Here 13C/12Csam is the carbon isotope ratio in a
sample and 13C/12Cstd is the carbon isotope ratio of V-PDB,
an established standard.) They used a flux-weighted average
value for the d13C ratio of CH4 entering the atmosphere and
allowed for a slight 13C enrichment in the resulting atmo-

[4] Resulting calculations by Stevens and Rust [1982]
helped apportion CH4 sources according to whether they
were relatively 13C-enriched or 13C-depleted with respect to
atmospheric CH4. Since then, additional measurements of
atmospheric d13C-CH4 have further constrained the CH4
budget, provided information on seasonal cycles in CH4
sources and sink processes, and helped understand changes
in CH4 mixing ratio [e.g., Wahlen et al., 1989; Quay et al.,
1991, 1999; Lowe et al., 1991, 1999; Thom et al., 1993;
Brenninkmeijer et al., 1995; Gupta et al., 1996; Tyler et al.,
1999; Miller et al., 2002; Morimoto et al., 2006]. In most
cases these studies included multiyear regularly scheduled
CH4 sampling of well-mixed background air for d13C-CH4
at a fixed surface site or sites and compared the atmospheric
data to available information on the d13C of CH4 sources
and the KIEs of multiple sink processes. Only a few studies
have reported substantial sets of measurements of dD in
atmospheric CH4 [Quay et al., 1999; Bergamaschi et al.,
2000; Rice et al., 2001, 2003]. Additional such atmospheric
measurements would clearly be useful in studying changes
in CH4 and its growth rate, just as measurements of 13C/12C
ratios in CH4 have proven to be.
[5] Here we present mixing and stable isotope ratio data
of both d13C and dD of CH4 from long-term air sample
collecting (approximately bimonthly) at two northern hemi-
spheric sites, Niwot Ridge, Colorado (NWT) and Montan˜a
de Oro, California (MDO). Measurement data from our air
samples (December 1994 to November 2001 at NWT,
January 1996 to December 2001 at MDO) show long-term
trends and seasonal cycles of mixing, and d13C and dD
isotope ratios at both locations. Analysis of the data
provides seasonal (winter/summer) temporal correlations,
and offsets (deviations from 6-month lag or lead times)
between isotope ratio values and CH4 mixing ratios. These
correlations illustrate the difference between the two isoto-
pic tracers and, in particular, the strong influence of CH4
sink reactions on atmospheric dD and the strong influence
of CH4 sources on atmospheric d13C, thereby also illustrat-
ing the value of multiple stable isotope constraints on the
CH4 budget.
[6] We also present mixing ratios of carbon monoxide
(CO) from the same air samples at both sites. In the
Northern Hemisphere (NH), long-term trends in CO mixing
ratio have gone from increasing (approximately 1980 to
1990) to decreasing (approximately 1991 to 2001) [Wotowa
et al., 2001; Novelli et al., 2003]. The consensus reason for
the decrease is that a decrease in NH fossil fuel emissions is
responsible. Carbon monoxide has a strong influence on the
oxidative capacity of the Earth’s atmosphere. Hydroxyl
radicals (OH), the most important oxidizing agent in the
atmosphere, react with CO rather quickly, giving a CO
lifetime of the order of a few months [Levy, 1971; Logan et
al., 1981; Levine et al., 1985]. As for other trace gases, a
knowledge of both the sources and sinks of tropospheric CO
is vital to understanding spatial and temporal qualities of
CO as well as for quantifying individual components of its
budget such as OH concentrations. For our study of CH4,
the CO mixing ratio data are of additional value because
they help determine whether any of our air samples of well-
mixed background air have been influenced by local or
regional sources.
2.
Site Descriptions
[7] Niwot Ridge, Colorado (40N, 105W), is on the
continental divide for North America. The air collection site
is located about 4 km southeast of NWT at an altitude of
approximately 3.15 km. It is situated on land owned by the
Mountain Research Station of the University of Colorado
and used as a Long-Term Ecological Research site by the
National Science Foundation. Air samples are collected for
us by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administra-
tion/Global Monitoring Division (NOAA/GMD) in Boul-
der, Colorado, through a collaborative agreement. Wind
patterns at NWT are dominated by westerlies [Barry,
1973; Haagenson, 1979] although at times upslope flow
from the south or southeast transports air from the Denver
metropolitan area (located about 70 km to the southeast) to
the site [Hahn, 1981; Johnson and Toth, 1982]. In our study
all samples were collected before noon in order to increase
the likelihood of sampling when the prevailing wind was
from the west. Samples taken in this manner have the best
chance of representing well-mixed background air from
over the western United States without interference from
regional contamination.
[8] Montan˜a de Oro State Park, a coastal site near San
Luis Obispo (35N, 121W), is protected from the city of
San Luis Obispo by low-lying foothills forming a ridge
running parallel to the coast. The city of Morro Bay is
northeast of the site, while the Diablo Canyon nuclear
power plant is about 2 km to the southeast. According to
the California Energy Commission [1985], which compiled
measurements at two wind stations near the MDO site
(Diablo Canyon, California, from years 1967 to 1981 and
Santa Maria, California, from years 1948 to 1978), average
annual wind speeds were about 16.1 and 12.7 km/hr,
respectively, at the two stations and were predominately
from the west and northwest. Additional data given for
Santa Maria indicates that the months from March to June
exhibited the highest winds, although only 20% or so above
the mean wind speed, while late afternoon to early evening
hours exhibited nearly twice the wind speed found for other
times of the day. Accordingly, our air samples from MDO
were collected in late afternoon or early evening during
periods when prevailing winds were from the west or
northwest.

3.2.
Mixing Ratio Analytical Procedures
[10] At UC Irvine, CH4 mixing ratios from canister and
cylinder samples were measured using a Hewlett Packard
5880A gas chromatograph (GC). The analytical system
included a 10-port valve, 2 mL sample loop, precolumn,
separation column, and flame ionization detector. The
separation column was 152 cm (3.18 mm o.d.) of molecular
sieve 5A while the precolumn was 25 cm (3.18 mm o.d.) of
the same material. Both columns were run isothermally at
100C using nitrogen carrier gas with the precolumn back
flushed contemporaneous to the latter part of the chromato-
graphic separation period. For measuring CO mixing ratios
we used a Shimadzu model 14A GC equipped with a 6-port
valve, 5 mL sample loop, separation column, and reduction
gas analyzer detector set at 275C (model RGD2, Trace
Analytical, Menlo Park, California). The separation column
was 152 cm (3.18 mm o.d.) of molecular sieve 5A set at
80C with a carrier gas of zero air. The separation column,
detector, and carrier gas cleaning columns were conditioned
between use as needed.
[11] Our mixing ratio working reference gases for CH4
and CO analyses are based on NOAA/GMD reference
scales for these gases and have been assigned values based
on our primary reference gas. Our primary reference gas
values of 1.903 ppm (CH4) and 134 ppb (CO) were directly
intercompared with NOAA reference standards for CH4
(CMDL83 scale [Lang et al., 1990]) and for CO (CMDL
scale WMO-88 [Novelli et al., 1991, 2003]). Our level of
precision for measurements are ±5 to 10 ppb (1s, n = 5)
for CH4 (approximately 0.25–0.50% uncertainty) and ±2–
4 ppb (1s, n = 5) for CO (approximately 1.0 to 2.0%
uncertainty).
[12] We also intercompared our CH4 mixing ratio results
to those of NOAA/GMD for all our NWT air samples. The
average difference between the values measured by NOAA
and by our lab was 3 ± 7 ppb (1s, n = 116), with NOAA
values higher. A significance test was made to establish the
confidence limits on the difference between the two means.
At the 95% confidence limit the 3 ppb difference was
insignificant (Dx(bar) = 8 ppb, n = 116). No adjustment
to NWT and MDO mixing ratio data presented here was
made based on the small difference just noted. Note,
however, that CH4 mole fractions in the newest NOAA
CH4 standard gas scale (NOAA04) are a factor of 1.0124 ±
0.0007 great than those expressed in the CMDL83 scale
[Dlugokencky et al., 2005].
3.3.
Isotope Ratio Analytical Procedures
[13] For d13C-CH4 analyses we used conventional dual
inlet isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) methodology.
In this method, air samples are processed using a combus-
tion vacuum line, a necessary step to separate CH4, CO and
CO2 from other constituents of the air sample and to convert
CH4 to CO2. In brief, a series of cryotraps at 196C
condense gases such as CO2 and water vapor, leaving CO
and CH4 in the air stream. The CO is then converted to CO2
using I2O5 on silica gel and trapped out at 196C before
oxidizing CH4 to CO2 and H2O in a combustion furnace.
Product CO2 from CH4 combustion can be separated from
the H2O by-product using an ethanol/dry ice cryotrap. For a
complete description of this procedure see Tyler et al.
[1999] and references therein. Methane samples prepared
as just described were then measured on our Finnigan MAT
model 252 IRMS instrument. Precision of measurement on
clean dry CO2 gas standards was ±0.01% (1s) for d13C and
±0.05% (1s) for d18O. The reproducibility of d13C mea-
surements from approximately 200-L each of replicate air
samples was ±0.05% (1s) when all possible errors associ-
ated with differences in sample canisters, sample pumping,
vacuum line processing, and isotope measurement were
taken into account.
[14] For dD-CH4 analyses we used our continuous flow
(cf) GC-IRMS Thermo-Finnigan model Delta Plus XL
instrument coupled to our custom-designed CH4 gas pre-
concentrator. A pyrolysis oven converts CH4 to H2 after its
separation from the air sample and before its detection by
the mass spectrometer. For a comprehensive description of
the CH4 sample preparation and analyses, see Rice et al.
[2001]. One procedural modification different than that
described by Rice et al. was the decrease in required sample
size from 120 to 63 mL of whole air, this serving to
decrease sample processing times without loss of precision.
Precision of measurement of dD-CH4 was ±1.1% (1s)
based on pooled statistics from replicate measurements (3)
of each sample. Long term precision and calibration drift
was monitored through the routine measurement of a
calibrated whole air sample. Combined, these uncertainties
were estimated at ±1.3% (1s).

CH4 for the period 1996–1999 (period with positive
growth) were 5.0 ± 1.8 ppb/yr (1s) at NWT and 5.3 ±
3.7 ppb/yr (1s) at MDO, in good agreement with the
growth rate of 5.4 ppb/yr (1s) reported for the 30–90N
latitudinal zone by NOAA/GMD for the years 1996–1999
[Dlugokencky et al., 2001]. Annual CH4 growth rates at
each site were determined by difference in mean values in
successive years. At NWT mean growth rates were 10, 1, 4,
10, 11, and 11 ppb for 1995–1996 through 2000–2001,
respectively. At MDO mean annual CH4 growth rates were
1, 2, 17, 3, and 6 ppb for 1996–1997 through 2000–
2001, respectively. While both NWT and MDO showed
positive change in CH4 mixing ratio over the entire duration
of our measurement record, data in Figures 1a and 2a show
a slowing of growth during 2000 at both sites and continu-
ing through the year 2001. The slowing of growth begin-
ning in 2000 was in keeping with results reported by
Simpson et al. [2002] whose data indicate globally averaged
negative growth in CH4 mixing ratio during 2000–2001,
unprecedented in modern (post industrial revolution) time
series of CH4 measurements.
[22] Because of large variability in mixing ratio as a result
of seasonal and irregular effects in these data relative to the
number of measurements, observed trends in CH4 growth
rate were not generally large enough to be statistically
significant at high levels of confidence (e.g., >20 ppb at
95% confidence interval). However, qualitatively both
NWT and MDO showed a large increase in CH4 growth
for the interval 1998–1999 compared to surrounding yearly
growth rate values, similar to that reported by Morimoto et
al. [2006]. Our record of growth rate fluctuations may
reflect the influence of the large ENSO occurrence during
1997–1998 and its reported effect on biomass burning and
enhanced CH4 release.

[39] The calculated carbon atmospheric KIE at each site is
larger than would be expected from a pure OH driven loss
process based on laboratory measurements of the carbon
KIE for atmospheric CH4 reaction with OH. The room
temperature k12/k13 value has been measured as either
1.0054 [Cantrell et al., 1990] or 1.0039 [Saueressig et al.,
2001]. However, the carbon KIEs observed at our sites are
consistent with observations by Allan et al. [2001b] in the
extratropical SH (approximately 1.013 based on data at
Scott Base, Antarctica and Baring Head New Zealand)
and Bergamaschi et al. [2000] in the midlatitude NH
(approximately 1.009 at Izan˜a, Tenerife). Although soil
uptake has a higher carbon KIE (1.022 [Tyler et al.,
1994]), the estimated upper limit for its global contribution
to the CH4 budget is only approximately 5% of the total
sink [e.g., Gupta et al., 1996]. Instead, marine boundary
layer Cl oxidation of CH4 (carbon KIE approximately 1.06)
has been identified as a potential explanation for the larger-
than-expected carbon KIE observed in d13C compared to
that from primarily OH loss [Gupta et al., 1996; Allan et al.,
2001b].
[40] For perspective, at 298 K, the value of the ratio of
reaction rate coefficients, kCl/kOH, for Cl and OH with CH4
is 15.9, while the ratio of KIE enrichment of CH4 oxidation
by Cl and OH at 298 K is either 12.2 (using 1.0054 for OH
KIE) or 16.9 (using 1.0039). Thus even a small amount of
marine boundary layer Cl may be an important contribution
to the overall carbon KIE of atmospheric CH4 in spite of its
much lesser abundance compared to OH. Our results from
NWT and MDO are consistent with a small but significant
amount of Cl oxidation of CH4 in the marine boundary
layer. Note that the magnitude of the Cl contribution to the
total KIE will impact the slope of the KIE line and add a
small off-axis distortion to the isotope phase ellipse [Allan
et al., 2001b].
[41] Seasonal variations in stratospheric-tropospheric ex-
change are also of potential influence to the observed
carbon atmospheric KIE. Because oxidation of CH4 by
OH, Cl, and O(1D) in the stratosphere enriches CH4 in
13C, return of stratospheric air to the troposphere enriches
tropospheric d13C-CH4 by as much as 1% [Gupta et al.,
1996; McCarthy et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2002]. Seasonal
fluctuations in stratospheric-tropospheric exchange, for ex-
ample after the springtime breakup of the polar vortex [e.g.,
Rex et al., 1999], may enrich observed tropospheric CH4
carbon isotopes annually, providing an inflection in residual
d13C-CH4 in Figures 3b, 4b, 5a, and 6a.

5.2.
A Simple Sink-Only Model for Methane
Seasonality
[46] Following the conceptual model of Bergamaschi et
al. [2000] and Allan et al. [2001a], we constructed a simple
time-dependent 1-box model to calculate the seasonal
effects on northern hemispheric CH4 from OH alone (i.e.,
the model assumes that sources are unchanging and are
input to maintain steady state over a year), similar to
equation (2) above. Observed deviations from model pre-
dictions can largely be attributed to seasonally variant
sources in the NH and transport associated with these
sources. If we assume a strictly aseasonal source function,
then variations in CH4 about its average value [CH4]o can
be described by the seasonally cycling OH mixing ratio
independent of sources and transport. For small perturba-
tions in CH4, d[CH4]0/dt = k[CH4]o[OH]0 where [OH]0 and
[CH4]0 are perturbations in OH and CH4 about their mean
values. Differential equations were solved for [CH4]0, d13C-

to be 8 years, perturbations in OH mixing ratio were derived

and Gierczak et al. [1997] at 277 K.
[47] This sink-only model predicts CH4 mixing and both

April. The observed phase lag between maximum OH

et al., 2001a]. Allan et al. [2001b] pointed out the impor-

d13C in the extratropical SH. We note that although includ-
ing Cl increases the amplitude by more than a factor of two,
the phase of the d13C cycle is changed by 1 month or less
[see Allan et al., 2001b, Figure 3].
