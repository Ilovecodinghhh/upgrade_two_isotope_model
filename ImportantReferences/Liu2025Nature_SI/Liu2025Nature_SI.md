## 1. Gaps in NOAA observation data

In this Supplementary section, we provide a comprehensive evaluation and further discussion of the gaps in NOAA observation data and their impacts on long-term trend detection of seasonal cycle amplitude.

We first provided the number of observations for each site from 1984 to 2020 (without missing data, there are 46 8-day flasks in a year) to show the spatial or temporal gaps in the observations (Supplementary Fig. 28). Some sites have started observations from 1994 (e.g., WIS), while some ceased observations after 2010 (e.g. STM). In our study, for the analysis of long-term trends in seasonal $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ amplitude, we selected data with each year having at least 23 8-day flasks (the grey squares in Supplementary Fig. 28 represent the gap years for the SCA analysis).

The observations at MLO are complete between 1984 and 2020, and show a significant increasing trend of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ SCA. To test the impacts of temporal gaps on the long-term trend detection, we added different numbers of artificial gap years at MLO. First, for each number of artificial gap years (from 1 year to 30 years), we have applied Monte Carlo method to take 1000 time series with randomly removing artificial gap years at MLO, and calculated the trends in SCA of these artificial time series. The proportion of the artificial time series with significant SCA trend $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ decreases with the increasing in artificial gap years (blue line in Supplementary Fig. 29). When the artificial temporal gaps are less than or equal to 7 years, over 95% of the time series show a significant SCA trend. Thus, the sites with temporal gap years $< = 7$ years could have reliable trends. For the latitudinal gradient of SCA trend analysis, if the 8 sites with more than 7 gap years are excluded, the SCA trend at the other 10 sites (<= 7 years) still significantly decreases with latitude (-1.0 [-1.5 – -0.5] ppb decade-1 per 10 degrees of latitude, $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ , compared with the result of all 18 sites (-0.8 [-1.3 – -0.4] ppb decade-1 per 10 degrees of latitude, $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ . For the spatial gaps, we find that at least one site for every 10 degrees of latitude north of $1 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { N }$ , well representing their latitudinal bands. There are seven sites in the Southern Hemisphere (Supplementary Fig. 2), and showing consistent increasing trends as the long-term observations at high quality site SPO. Thus, these gaps in the observational data have limited impacts on the findings in our study.

## 2. Uncertainties of wetland emissions

For evaluating the uncertainties of wetland emissions, we have compiled 31 datasets of wetland emissions, including modelling results of ORCHIDEE, LPJ-EOSIM1 and from Global Carbon Project2,3 (GCP-CH4), WETCHARTS4 , and atmospheric inversions from CAMS5 , as well as upscaling wetland methane emissions $\mathrm { ( U p C H _ { 4 } ) ^ { 6 } }$ and Boreal-Arctic wetland emissions from the causality-guided machine learning model7 .

In the northern high latitudes, 29 out of 31 datasets indicate an increasing trend of wetland emissions (median: 0.3 Tg $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \ \mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 }$ , ranging from -0.3 to 1.6 Tg CH4 $\mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 } ;$ ; Supplementary Fig. 30), supporting the increased wetland emissions in the northern high latitudes inferred from the decreasing trend of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ SCA in this study. Moreover, 28 datasets show increase in tropical wetland emissions after 2000 (median: 2.5 Tg $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 }$ , ranging from -0.9 to 11.3 Tg $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \ \mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 } )$ . The increase in emissions (amplitude) from northern and southern tropical wetlands could lower atmospheric $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ SCA, which contradicts to the observed increasing trend of SCA in the tropics (Fig. 1). The increasing trends of wetland emissions in northern high latitudes $\mathrm { ( 0 . 9 T g }$ $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \ \mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 } )$ and tropics (2.6 Tg $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 } \ \mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 } )$ from ORCHIDEE are close to ensemble mean increase rate of 0.6 Tg CH4 $\mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 }$ and 3.3 $\mathrm { T g }$ CH4 $\mathrm { y r } ^ { - 2 }$ from northern high wetland and tropical wetland (Supplementary Fig. 30), respectively.

## 3. Uncertainties introduced by offline OH concentration

For evaluating the uncertainties introduced by offline OH concentration, we have quantified the differences between the offline OH concentration forced by the observed $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ concentration (OH baseline; Supplementary Fig. 31) and the OH concentration forced by the $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ concentration in our T1 experiments simulated with OH concentrations from INCA (T1-INCA) and GEOS-Chem (T1-GEOSChem), respectively. We found that both the OH concentrations forced by $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ from T1-INCA and T1-GEOSChem are close to the OH baseline (Supplementary Fig. 31), with discrepancies lower than 0.7% in these two periods 1984-1989 (-0.3% for T1-INCA and 0.1% for T1-GEOSChem) and 2015-2020 (-0.1% for T1-INCA and 0.7% for T1-GEOSChem). Meanwhile, the discrepancies of 0.7% in OH concentrations would lead to an error of \~1.3 ppb (0.7% multiple the SOH) in the simulation of atmospheric $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ concentration. This indicates that the uncertainty introduced by the offline and online OH has a minimal impact on the results.

## 4. Seasonal cycle amplitude of other trace gases and MALTA model

We have collected available data for 42 compounds (Table S3), with a span of lifetimes, from NOAA’ Long-term Observations of Greenhouse gases and Ozone-depleting Substances (LOGOS; https://gml.noaa.gov/hats/) and from the Advanced Global Atmospheric Gases Experiment (AGAGE; https://www-air.larc.nasa.gov/missions/agage/data). Several compounds have been ruled out following three criteria as below.

1) For evaluating long-term trends in SCA, 23 out of 42 compounds with continuous observations less than 20 years are excluded. Although the observations of HFC-125 and HCFC-124 from AGAGE started since 1998, they have discontinued measurements due to the replacement of instruments in 2000s. These two compounds were excluded to be on the safe side.

2) Then, we excluded the compounds, whose sink is not dominated by tropospheric OH concentration. Specifically, the stratospheric photolysis is the primary sink of CFC-11, CFC-12, CFC-113, $\mathrm { C C l } _ { 4 } .$ , halon-1211, halon-1301 and halon-2402 (see Fig 3.5 in Ko et al. (ref.8 )). The lifetime of $\mathrm { C } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 4 }$ is strongly affected by the tropospheric Cl sink (101 days without Cl sink; 68 days with Cl sink)9 . The ocean and soil uptake contributes to over 50% of total sink of $\mathrm { C H _ { 3 } B r ^ { 1 0 , 1 1 } }$ , while multiple lines of evidence support uptake by plants as the dominant removal mechanism of atmospheric $\mathrm { C O S } ^ { 1 2 , 1 3 }$

3) Finally, to mitigate the impact of emissions on the changes of SCA, the compounds with strong seasonal natural or anthropogenic emissions are also excluded. There is a welldocumented seasonal variation in global anthropogenic emissions of HCFC-22 and HFC-134a, with the summer emissions of these refrigerants being two to three times higher than in winter14. Furthermore, the seasonally varying anthropogenic emission has been shown to contribute to the seasonal cycle of atmospheric HCFC-152a15. The global emissions of CHCl3 are mainly attributed to natural emissions16 (90.5%), including oceanic flux, soil and termites. Similarly, the global emissions of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C l }$ are presumably caused by large tropical terrestrial sources, while anthropogenic emissions only contributed to 5.4% of global emissions17. The inversion results suggest significant seasonal variations in the natural $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C l }$ sources17.

As a result, four compounds $\mathrm { ( C H _ { 2 } C l _ { 2 } , C H _ { 3 } C C l _ { 3 } }$ , HCFC-141b and HCFC-142b) are applied for the SCA analysis. The seasonal cycle of CH2Cl2 is mainly due to its reaction with the OH radical, with maxima in late winter/early spring and corresponding minima in late summer or early autumn18. With regard to the emissions of ${ \mathrm { C H } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { C l } } _ { 2 }$ it has a range of industrial applications and roughly 90% of total emissions have been estimated to be anthropogenic19, and no significant seasonal variation in industrial emissions has been reported20. The emissions of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C C l } _ { 3 }$ have declined substantially due to the revised and amended Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer19, suggesting that the seasonal variations of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C C l } _ { 3 }$ are dominated by the OH sink. Given the lack of available references/datasets showing the seasonality in HCFC-141b and HCFC-142b emissions, these two compounds are also included in our analysis.

The changes in OH sink of each compound, contributed by both its atmospheric concentration and OH concentration, may potentially play a key role on the SCA. To quantify the contributions of OH concentration to the observed SCA, we used a zonally averaged global atmospheric transport model $\mathrm { M A L T A } ^ { 2 1 }$ to attribute the changes in SCA of these four compounds. The horizontal resolution of MALTA is every $1 0 ^ { \circ }$ in latitude and the bounds of altitude are at 30 levels (29 layers) from 1000 hPa to 10hPa. For each compound, the MALTA is forced by dynamic transport and mereological fields (MERRA2; ref. 21) with varying OH concentration from GEOS-Chem (M1 simulation). The emissions are assumed to be distributed latitudinally by Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in a given year, and without seasonal variations. The year-to-year emissions are iteratively adjusted by gradient descent to ensure the annual atmospheric concentration from simulation are consistent with that from observations at MLO (Eq. S1).

$$
E _ { n + 1 , y } = E _ { n , y } - \left( C _ { n , y } - C _ { M L O } \right) * c f * g\tag{Eq. (S1}
$$

Where $C _ { M L O }$ is the observed atmospheric concentration at MLO. $E _ { n , y }$ and $C _ { n , y }$ is the global emission and simulated $1 0 { - } 3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { N }$ average atmospheric concentration in the third layers (\~2900m) for each year $y ,$ in the nth iteration. The $c f$ is the conversion factor from emissions to concentrations and $g$ is a constant factor of decreasing rate. We performed 5 years spin-up for short-lived gas (CH2Cl2) and 20 years spin-up for the other three gases. We also performed M2 simulation with fixed OH concentration, while emissions and atmospheric concentration involved in the oxidation reaction for each compound are kept the same as M1 simulation. Then we applied SSA frequency decomposition to extract the seasonal cycle of atmospheric concentrations of these four compounds from observations and simulations. The first and last year of the SCA are excluded in the trend analysis.

Without seasonal variations in emissions, the seasonal cycle of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C C l } _ { 3 }$ from our simulations show a good match to the SCA from observations (Supplementary Fig. 32). However, the simulations of HCFC-141b and HCFC-142b fail to replicate the observed seasonal variations in atmospheric concentration. The simulated seasonal atmospheric concentration of HCFC-141b and HCFC-142b peaks in May and then decreases, driven by an increase in the atmospheric sink of OH radicals, while the seasonal concentration from observations increases in summer (June, July and August). The increase in the seasonal concentration is likely to be contributed by the seasonality in emissions, with higher emissions in summer as components of some refrigerant blends. Thus, we deduce that the SCA of HCFC-141b and HCFC-142b are dominated by the emissions, rather than the OH sink.

The seasonal cycles of CH2Cl2 and CH3CCl3 are dominated by the OH sink. The M1 simulation could capture the seasonal cycle of these two compounds and the changes of SCA over the past two decades (Supplementary Fig. 33). The M2 simulation with fixed OH concentration show a worse performance on capturing the trends in SCA than M1 simulation (Supplementary Fig. 33), indicating OH concentration has contributed to the trends in SCA of these two compounds. The increasing trend of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ SCA from our simulations (0.563 ppt $\mathrm { y r ^ { - 1 } } )$ is contributed by both rising CH2Cl2 concentration (0.430 ppt $\mathrm { y r } ^ { - 1 }$ ; 76%) and increasing OH concentration (0.123 ppt $\mathrm { y r } ^ { - 1 }$ ; 24%). The decreasing trend of $\mathrm { l o g ( C H _ { 3 } C C l _ { 3 } }$ SCA) (-0.1768 ppt $\mathrm { y r } ^ { - 1 } )$ is dominated by the decline of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 3 } \mathrm { C C l } _ { 3 }$ atmospheric concentration (-0.1770 ppt $\mathrm { y r ^ { - 1 } }$ ; 101%), from 120.7 ppt in 1994 to 1.4 ppt in 2020, partly offset by the enhanced OH concentration (0.002 ppt $\mathrm { y r ^ { - 1 } }$ ; 1%).

Overall, the coincident evidences from $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ SCA and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ SCA suggest an increase in the tropospheric OH concentration. The positive correlations between the interannual variabilities of $\mathrm { C H } _ { 4 }$ amplitude and $\mathrm { C H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { C l } _ { 2 }$ amplitude at MLO $( \mathrm { r } = 0 . 5 0 - 0 . 6 1$ ， ${ \mathfrak { p } } = 0 . 0 0 - 0 . 0 2 )$ and KUM $( \mathrm { r } = 0 . 3 9 - 0 . 4 9 )$ $\mathsf { p } = 0 . 0 5 - 0 . 1 1$ ; Supplementary Fig. 27) further support that the increasing trends in SCA of these gases are likely both driven by changes in OH sink, especially the OH concentration.

It is worth noting that the simple atmospheric transport model MALTA used here only provides a perspective on the contributions of OH concentration to SCA, considering MALTA’s horizontal resolution is $1 0 ^ { \circ }$ in latitude. Moreover, the latitudinal distributions and temporal trends of emissions have large uncertainties22,23. The seasonal cycle of trace gases provides a valuable opportunity for better understanding the trends in OH concentration, but further research is needed to reduce the uncertainties (e.g., emission inventories with spatial distribution, seasonality of emissions etc.).

Supplementary Figure 6. Mechanisms and factorial experiments for explaining the changes in seasonal $\mathbf { C H _ { 4 } }$ amplitude. Increasing emissions amplitude could lead to a lower CH4 seasonal amplitude, while increasing OH sink amplitude could result in a higher CH4 seasonal amplitude (a). Atmospheric transport could contribute to a decrease of amplitude via more summer CH4 mass flux from high latitudes, and vice versa. We designed three transportation experiments to quantify the contributions of emissions, sinks and atmospheric transport to the trends in SCA (b): T1 experiment with varying emissions $( \mathrm { E _ { v a r y } } )$ and sink $( \mathrm { S } _ { \mathrm { v a r y } } ) { \mathrm { ; } }$ ; T2 experiment with emissions (E1984) and sink (S1984) held constant at their 1984 levels; T3 experiment with varying emissions but sink held at its 1984 level. Thus, the contribution of the sink can be estimated as the SCA in T1 minus that in T3 and the contribution of emissions can be estimated as the SCA in T3 minus that in T2. The symbols used in this figure are from Integration and Application Network (ian.umces.edu/media-library) under Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-SA 4.0).
